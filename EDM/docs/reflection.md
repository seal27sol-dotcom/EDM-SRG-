Working through Project Savanna changed how I think about data transformation in Uganda. At first, the case looks like a database consolidation problem: many POS databases, a separate e-commerce platform, loyalty, CRM, Odoo, Excel, and manual mobile-money files. The more important lesson is that technology choices are downstream of institutions and everyday work. A store may lose network access for hours; a cashier still has customers waiting. A supplier may send a workbook because that is the only exchange format that works. A finance officer may consolidate spreadsheets because no governed alternative arrives before the Board meeting. A solution that assumes reliable connectivity and a specialist engineering team would describe a different retailer, not SRG.

That context makes a hub-and-spoke approach more credible than an immediate central database. The local store process needs to continue, while a durable outbox makes delayed integration observable and replayable. I would measure the actual outage window, queue growth, duplicate replay rate, and staff burden before choosing a cloud-service contract. Uganda's digital transformation depends on useful infrastructure, but it also depends on designs that work through uneven electricity, connectivity, skills, and procurement capacity. A lower-cost product is not necessarily cheaper if four IT staff cannot operate it or a vendor cannot explain how to recover data after an outage.

The customer identity problem has an ethical dimension that a matching score cannot erase. If a false merge combines two people's purchase history or loyalty balance, one person may receive another person's points, marketing, or customer-service response. A shared family phone is not proof that two records belong to one individual; a recycled phone number can make yesterday's deterministic key belong to someone else tomorrow. Matching therefore needs evidence, a conservative threshold, human review for conflict, an audit trail, and a way to reverse a decision without losing either source record. The algorithm should avoid protected attributes and proxies where possible, but even district and name similarity can disadvantage language communities or people whose names are transliterated differently. I would evaluate false-match and missed-match rates by relevant groups only where lawful and ethically justified, and use that review to change rules rather than to classify people unfairly.

Loyalty consent also deserves more care than a single checkbox. A customer can reasonably agree to collect points for purchases without agreeing to promotional messaging or an unrelated analytics purpose. The system should record what was explained, the purpose, the notice version, the time, the source, and a withdrawal event. Service and marketing purposes should be separated in the interface and in downstream rules. Data minimization matters: if a points balance can be managed with a pseudonymous customer key, the report layer does not need an unmasked phone number or national identifier. Retention must reflect business and legal needs, but keeping data forever because storage is cheap is not neutral; it increases the harm of theft and makes old preferences look current.

The footfall sensor use case raises a different risk. Aggregate entrance counts can help a store plan staffing or understand demand, but linking a sensor event to a loyalty member would change a low-resolution operational measure into tracking. Face images, persistent device identifiers, and precise trajectories are not needed for the assignment's stated operational purpose. Aggregating at the edge, retaining only counts and time buckets, posting a clear notice, and testing whether re-identification is possible are better safeguards than relying on a general privacy policy. The fact that technology can connect footfall and purchases does not mean the organization should do so.

This work also made me more skeptical of dashboards that present a number without its lineage. A monthly active-customer figure depends on how an active transaction is defined, whether voids and returns count, how duplicates are resolved, what time zone defines a month, and which sources arrived before the refresh. A clear chart is not a trustworthy metric by itself. I included a lineage path and used explicit caveats on the synthetic dashboard. I would not claim churn, stock optimization, or fraud from the generated data because the required histories, stock snapshots, margins, and payment-event evidence are absent. A consultant's responsibility includes stating when the data cannot answer the question.
